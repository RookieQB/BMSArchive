Sabdariffa gossypiifolia

Roselle / Hibiscus sabdariffa

Research areas: Blood Pressure Mechanisms, Cardiovascular Research

Overview · Clinical data and safety · Sources

Research overview

Historical use

Roselle calyces, reported in the literature as Hibiscus sabdariffa, are used as sour beverages and in traditional medicine in Africa and Asia; this history is distinct from clinical evidence [2][6].

Modern research context

Roselle calyx infusions have been studied for blood-pressure outcomes, with substantial preparation and study-quality limitations [1][2]. In a double-blind trial of 65 adults aged 30–70 years with prehypertension or mild hypertension who were not taking blood-pressure medicines, a six-week calyx-tea regimen reduced systolic pressure more than placebo; the diastolic difference was not significant [1]. A review of 17 chronic trials found a systolic-pressure signal but very high heterogeneity (I²=95% in the placebo comparison); this supports a conservative Level B for the studied blood-pressure outcomes, not proven cardiovascular-event prevention or medication equivalence [2]. A 25-man acute crossover study improved flow-mediated dilation without a significant blood-pressure reduction [4].

Adverse effects

Mild, transient gastrointestinal symptoms were reported in one trial summarized by the review; short trials and incomplete reporting cannot establish long-term safety or rare-event rates [2]. Hypersensitivity is a regulatory contraindication [6].

Contraindications

Hypersensitivity to roselle or related Malvaceae is listed by EDA [6]. Low blood pressure and concurrent blood-pressure treatment warrant precautionary clinical assessment because additive effects are plausible [2][6]. Medicinal use in children is insufficiently characterized in these sources. EDA includes a precaution to stop two weeks before planned surgery because of possible glucose effects; a clinical perioperative benefit of that interval has not been established [6].

Clinical and phytochemical data

Used part

Fleshy red calyces, usually dried and infused in water; tea and concentrated extracts have different constituent exposures. Clinical sources use the synonym Hibiscus sabdariffa [1][5][6].

Primary active compounds

  • Delphinidin-3-sambubioside (anthocyanin) [2][3]

  • Cyanidin-3-sambubioside (anthocyanin) [2][3]

  • Phenolic acids including gallic acid [4]

  • Hibiscus acid and related organic acids [6]

Mechanism of action

Animal and in-vitro work summarized in the review investigates angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE), vascular calcium influx and endothelial nitric-oxide signaling; these hypotheses do not establish clinical ACE inhibition or explain all human effects [2]. Human flow-mediated dilation improved in one small acute trial, but nitric-oxide and blood-pressure changes were not significant [4].

Pharmacokinetics

Absorption

In six healthy volunteers given a single Hibiscus extract, measured intact anthocyanins peaked in plasma at a median 1.5 hours. These abstract-level, compound-specific findings cannot describe absorption of the complete botanical or establish calyx-product dosing [3].

Distribution

Human tissue distribution and protein binding of complete calyx extracts were not established in the selected sources [2][3].

Metabolism

A small human crossover trial detected circulating gallic acid and methylated phenolic acids after an aqueous calyx drink; complete metabolic pathways and clinically relevant CYP effects remain unresolved [4].

Excretion

In six volunteers, 0.018% of the administered total anthocyanins was recovered as measured intact anthocyanins in urine within seven hours. This is not total recovery of all metabolites; extract-level clearance and impairment adjustments are unknown [3].

Safety and interactions

Drug interactions

Additive hypotension with antihypertensive drugs is a precautionary pharmacodynamic concern [2][6]. EDA also flags chloroquine, simvastatin and diabetes medicines, but this extraction has not independently verified the underlying clinical interaction studies; direction and magnitude must not be assumed [6]. A hydrochlorothiazide interaction described in the review was in rats and is not an established human interaction [2].

Toxicity

No reliable long-term human toxicity threshold is established. Short trial tolerability and animal toxicity results do not establish safety during chronic concentrated-extract use [2].

Special precautions

Pregnancy

Insufficient human pregnancy safety data; EDA does not recommend medicinal use during pregnancy [6].

Lactation

Milk transfer and infant safety are unknown; EDA does not recommend medicinal use during breastfeeding [6].

Hepatic impairment

No validated hepatic-impairment dosing or safety studies identified in the selected sources; concentrated-extract exposure requires individual clinical assessment [2][6].

Renal impairment

No validated renal-impairment dosing data identified in these sources; potential blood-pressure and diuretic effects warrant individualized assessment with concurrent medicines. This is a precautionary inference, not an impairment-specific clinical finding [2][6].
