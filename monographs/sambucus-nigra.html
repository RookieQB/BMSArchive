Sambucus nigra

Elderberry / Black elder

Research areas: Viral Mechanisms Research, Immunological Research, Inflammatory Pathways

Overview · Clinical data and safety · Sources

Research overview

Historical use

Sambucus nigra has been used medicinally across Europe and the Americas for millennia. Flowers (Flos Sambuci) were employed to reduce fever, soothe respiratory catarrh, and treat skin disorders; ripe berries (Fructus Sambuci) were made into syrups, wines, and decoctions for influenza, colds, and constipation. The plant features in the materia medica of Hippocrates, Dioscorides, and Galen, and was known as the 'medicine chest of the country people' in 17th-century England. Indigenous North American peoples used elderberry preparations for infections, pain relief, and as a purgative.

Modern research context

Elderberry extracts are among the most clinically studied herbal antivirals. A 2026 ethnopharmacological review confirms activity against influenza, SARS-CoV-2, RSV, and other RNA viruses through inhibition of viral surface proteins hemagglutinin and neuraminidase [1, 8]. In vitro studies demonstrate synergistic antiviral effects when combined with iota-carrageenan [4] and quinine [5]. A 2026 randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial found significant reductions in paediatric respiratory tract infection frequency and severity with an elderberry-containing supplement [3]. Reviews document additional cardioprotective [2], antidiabetic, antioxidant, and neuroprotective activities [1, 6]. A 2025 transcriptomic study in overweight adults showed one week of elderberry juice significantly altered gene expression toward enhanced metabolic flexibility and insulin signalling [7].

Adverse effects

Processed elderberry preparations are generally well-tolerated at standard doses. Rare gastrointestinal effects (nausea, vomiting) with excessive intake. Raw unripe berries, seeds, bark, and leaves contain the cyanogenic glycoside sambunigrin, causing nausea and emesis if consumed raw; heating or processing eliminates this compound [1, 6]. Theoretical immunostimulatory activity may aggravate autoimmune conditions.

Contraindications

Raw, unprocessed plant material contraindicated in all populations. Caution warranted in patients on immunosuppressants (calcineurin inhibitors, corticosteroids) due to opposing immunostimulatory activity [1]. Avoid in known hypersensitivity to Sambucus species or the Adoxaceae family.

Clinical and phytochemical data

Used part

Ripe berries (Fructus Sambuci), flowers (Flos Sambuci)

Primary active compounds

  • Cyanidin-3-glucoside (anthocyanin)

  • Cyanidin-3-sambubioside (anthocyanin)

  • Quercetin

  • Rutin

  • Kaempferol

  • Chlorogenic acid

  • Sambunigrin (cyanogenic glycoside, raw plant only)

Mechanism of action

Antiviral activity mediated through direct binding to viral hemagglutinin and neuraminidase surface proteins, blocking host-cell sialic acid receptor recognition and viral entry [4, 5, 8]. Anthocyanins activate TLR4/NF-κB and MAPK signalling, stimulating innate immune cytokine production (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β) [1]. Antioxidant activity via Nrf2/HO-1 pathway induction and direct free-radical scavenging by phenolic hydroxyl groups [6]. Cardioprotective effects involve eNOS upregulation, inhibition of LDL oxidation, and attenuation of NF-κB-driven vascular inflammation [2].

Pharmacokinetics

Absorption

No human pharmacokinetic data are available for this species. The following is derived from preclinical animal studies and in vitro data only, and may not reflect human pharmacology. — Elderberry anthocyanins are absorbed in the gastrointestinal tract with low absolute oral bioavailability. Cyanidin-3-glucoside and cyanidin-3-sambubioside demonstrate rapid absorption in animal models; peak plasma concentrations are reached within 0.5–1 hour post-administration. Bioavailability is limited (<5%) due to extensive presystemic metabolism by intestinal epithelium and colonic microbiota, converting anthocyanins to simpler phenolic acids [1, 6].

Distribution

No human pharmacokinetic data are available for this species. The following is derived from preclinical animal studies and in vitro data only, and may not reflect human pharmacology. — In rodent models, anthocyanins and their phenolic acid metabolites distribute widely to vascular tissues, liver, kidneys, and bladder epithelium. Plasma protein binding of elderberry flavonoids (quercetin, rutin) exceeds 80–90%, predominantly to albumin [6].

Metabolism

No human pharmacokinetic data are available for this species. The following is derived from preclinical animal studies and in vitro data only, and may not reflect human pharmacology. — Cyanidin glycosides undergo rapid hydrolysis by intestinal β-glucosidases and gut microbiota, releasing cyanidin aglycone, which is further ring-opened to protocatechuic acid and phloroglucinaldehyde. Phase II hepatic conjugation (glucuronidation, sulfation, O-methylation) generates circulating metabolites including quercetin-3-glucuronide and isorhamnetin [1, 6].

Excretion

No human pharmacokinetic data are available for this species. The following is derived from preclinical animal studies and in vitro data only, and may not reflect human pharmacology. — Elimination is primarily renal for phase II-conjugated phenolic metabolites; <1% of intact anthocyanins is recovered in urine. Biliary excretion accounts for elimination of high-molecular-weight glucuronide and sulfate conjugates [6].

Safety and interactions

Drug interactions

Lack of clinical studies prevents interaction grading. Unknown safety profile with concomitant drug use.

Toxicity

Acute oral toxicity of processed elderberry preparations is low. Standardised elderberry extract has been administered across multiple clinical trials without hepatotoxic or haematological adverse signals [3]. Raw plant material containing sambunigrin is toxic; cooking or commercial processing eliminates this risk [1, 6].

Special precautions

Pregnancy

Insufficient clinical safety data. Processed berry extracts at normal dietary doses are likely low-risk, but medicinal-dose supplements are not recommended during pregnancy due to absence of controlled trials.

Lactation

Excretion of elderberry constituents in human breast milk is unstudied. Medicinal-dose supplements not recommended.

Hepatic impairment

No specific clinical data. Standard hepatic precautions apply; avoid concentrated extracts in severe hepatic impairment.

Renal impairment

No specific data. Standard precautions apply at normal supplement doses.
