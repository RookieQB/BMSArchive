Linum usitatissimum

Flax / Linseed

For educational and scientific research only. Not medical advice. Independently verify claims against the cited primary sources. Consult a qualified healthcare professional before using any plant or fungal preparation.

Research areas: Gastrointestinal Research, Blood Pressure Mechanisms

Overview · Clinical data and safety · Sources

Research overview

Historical use

Linseed has long been used as a bulk-forming laxative; EMA distinguishes well-established use of seed for habitual constipation from traditional demulcent use for mild gastrointestinal discomfort [5].

Modern research context

Flaxseed products have been studied for bowel function and blood-pressure outcomes, with seed, oil and lignan extracts representing different interventions [1][2][5]. A 2023 meta-analysis of 33 trials (2427 participants) reported modest pooled blood-pressure reductions but very high heterogeneity (I² above 92%); the archive uses conservative Level B for the studied pressure outcomes rather than generalizing to every product [1]. An earlier analysis also found formulation-dependent results [2]. EMA recognizes seed as a bulk laxative, while demulcent use is traditional (Level T); regulatory well-established use is not itself an archive Level A clinical-trial score [5]. A separate triple-blind trial studied flaxseed regimens for 12 weeks, but its abstract gives inconsistent participant totals, so it is not used for a precise sample-size or effect claim [4].

Adverse effects

EMA lists flatulence as common and hypersensitivity including anaphylaxis-like reactions as very rare. Insufficient fluid can cause choking, oesophageal blockage or intestinal obstruction; excessive seed exposure can cause abdominal discomfort and flatulence [5]. NCCIH also notes bloating, fullness and diarrhea with higher exposures [6].

Contraindications

EMA contraindicates hypersensitivity, swallowing/throat disorders, intestinal narrowing or obstruction/ileus, megacolon and certain unexplained bowel symptoms, including rectal bleeding or persistent sudden bowel-habit changes. Medicinal use under age 12 is not recommended; EMA also cautions against use in women with hormone-dependent tumours [5]. A validated perioperative washout interval was not identified.

Clinical and phytochemical data

Used part

Dried ripe seeds, whole/broken or milled, and seed mucilage. Seed oil lacks the same bulk-forming fibre exposure; purified lignan extracts have a separate pharmacokinetic and safety evidence base [3][5][6].

Primary active compounds

  • Mucilage polysaccharides and dietary fibre [5]

  • Secoisolariciresinol diglycoside (SDG; lignan) [3]

  • Alpha-linolenic acid in seed oil [6]

Mechanism of action

For bowel effects, water-binding mucilage increases intestinal content volume and softens stool, with stretch-related effects on transit; this is a local bulk mechanism described by EMA, not a receptor-specific systemic action [5]. Blood-pressure mechanisms are not conclusively assigned to a single constituent in the human meta-analyses [1][2].

Pharmacokinetics

Absorption

In healthy postmenopausal women given purified flax lignan extracts, secoisolariciresinol peaked at approximately 5–7 hours; enterodiol and enterolactone appeared later. These results describe SDG-derived compounds, not whole-seed mucilage or seed oil [3].

Distribution

Human tissue distribution of complete seed products was not established in the selected sources; purified-lignan serum exposure does not establish all tissue concentrations [3].

Metabolism

SDG is hydrolyzed to secoisolariciresinol and converted by intestinal bacteria to enterodiol and enterolactone [3]. Some seed bulk material is fermented to short-chain fatty acids while some is passed in stool [5].

Excretion

Human SDG studies measured urinary lignans and reported approximate serum half-lives of 4.8 hours for secoisolariciresinol, 9.4 hours for enterodiol and 13.2 hours for enterolactone; these cannot define whole-seed clearance or impairment dosing [3].

Safety and interactions

Drug interactions

EMA warns that linseed can delay intestinal absorption of medicines and specifies separation of at least half to one hour. Co-use with motility-suppressing medicines such as opioids or loperamide requires medical supervision because of obstruction risk [5]. NCCIH describes anticoagulant/antiplatelet interactions as theoretical, not established clinical bleeding interactions [6].

Toxicity

NCCIH advises against eating raw or unripe seeds because of potentially toxic constituents; seed preparation matters [6]. EMA identifies obstruction risk when fluid intake is inadequate and incomplete reproductive/genotoxicity/carcinogenicity testing. No universal long-term toxicity threshold can be inferred from short supplementation trials [5][6].

Special precautions

Pregnancy

Medicinal pregnancy safety is not established and EMA does not recommend use; NCCIH notes inconclusive possible pregnancy risk. This does not equate medicinal preparations with ordinary food exposure [5][6].

Lactation

Milk transfer and infant safety are insufficiently characterized; EMA does not recommend medicinal use while breastfeeding [5][6].

Hepatic impairment

No validated hepatic-impairment adjustment for medicinal seed or lignan extract was established in the selected sources [3][5].

Renal impairment

No validated renal-impairment adjustment is established. Fluid-dependent bulk use requires individual assessment when fluid intake is restricted; this is a precaution rather than a tested dosing rule [5].
