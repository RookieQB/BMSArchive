Grifola frondosa

Maitake / Hen-of-the-Woods

Research areas: Immunological Research, Glucose & Metabolic Research, Cellular & Immune Studies

Overview · Clinical data and safety · Sources

Research overview

Historical use

Used in Traditional Chinese and Japanese medicine as a tonic and food. Known in Japan as 'Maitake' (dancing mushroom), legend holds that people danced with joy upon finding it in the wild. Used historically for fatigue, diabetes-like symptoms, and immune support. Cultivated commercially since the 1970s in Japan.

Modern research context

D-fraction and MD-fraction (beta-1,6/1,3-glucan protein complexes) have shown immunomodulatory and anti-tumour effects in preclinical studies and small human trials; a meta-analysis confirmed polysaccharide anti-tumour activity [1]. Neurotrophic properties, including support of nerve growth factor (NGF) signalling, are under investigation [2]. Anti-proliferative activity in keratinocyte-related pathways via MAPK inhibition has been documented [3]. Maitake is included in reviews of edible mushroom anti-cancer potential across multiple cancer types [4]. Neuroprotective bioactivities are an emerging research focus [5].

Adverse effects

Generally well tolerated. Mild GI symptoms. Risk of hypoglycaemia with antidiabetic medications. Theoretical bleeding risk.

Contraindications

Antidiabetic medications — risk of additive hypoglycaemia. Anticoagulants — theoretical risk. Immunosuppressants — pharmacodynamic antagonism.

Clinical and phytochemical data

Used part

Fruiting body

Primary active compounds

  • D-fraction / MD-fraction (beta-1,6/1,3-glucan-protein complex)

  • SX-fraction (alpha-glucan)

  • Polysaccharides

  • Ergosterol

  • Phospholipids

Mechanism of action

D-fraction binds Dectin-1 and TLR-2 on macrophages and dendritic cells, activating innate and adaptive immune responses; increases NK cell and CD4+ T-cell activity. Anti-tumour mechanism involves upregulation of TNF-α, IL-12, and direct tumour cytotoxicity. SX-fraction increases insulin receptor sensitivity via PPAR-γ activation, reducing blood glucose and insulin resistance.

Pharmacokinetics

Absorption

No human pharmacokinetic data are available for this species. The following is derived from preclinical animal studies and in vitro data only, and may not reflect human pharmacology. — Beta-glucan fractions have limited oral bioavailability; partially absorbed in low-molecular-weight fractions and active at gut mucosal level. SX-fraction absorption profile not fully characterised.

Distribution

No human pharmacokinetic data are available for this species. The following is derived from preclinical animal studies and in vitro data only, and may not reflect human pharmacology. — D-fraction distributes to GALT, spleen, and lymph nodes in animal models; higher affinity for lymphoid tissue and tumour microenvironment. SX-fraction distribution to peripheral tissues (muscle, liver, adipose) demonstrated in rodent models. Human tissue distribution data are not available.

Metabolism

No human pharmacokinetic data are available for this species. The following is derived from preclinical animal studies and in vitro data only, and may not reflect human pharmacology. — Intestinal microbial fermentation of polysaccharides produces short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs). Absorbed fractions undergo hepatic metabolism.

Excretion

No human pharmacokinetic data are available for this species. The following is derived from preclinical animal studies and in vitro data only, and may not reflect human pharmacology. — Primarily fecal; smaller metabolites via renal route.

Safety and interactions

Drug interactions

Lack of clinical studies prevents interaction grading. Unknown safety profile with concomitant drug use.

Toxicity

LD50 >3 g/kg (oral, rodents). No organ toxicity in chronic animal studies. Human trials up to 6 g/day showed no serious adverse events.

Special precautions

Pregnancy

Insufficient clinical data. Avoid concentrated extracts during pregnancy.

Lactation

No data on breast milk excretion. Dietary amounts likely safe; supplemental doses — insufficient data.

Hepatic impairment

No hepatotoxicity documented. Standard monitoring recommended.

Renal impairment

No specific risk identified. No dose adjustment data available.
