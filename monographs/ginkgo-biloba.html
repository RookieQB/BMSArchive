Ginkgo biloba

Ginkgo / Maidenhair tree

Research areas: Cognitive Function Research, Neurological & Cognitive Research, Cardiovascular System

Overview · Clinical data and safety · Sources

Research overview

Historical use

One of the oldest living tree species on Earth (>200 million years old), revered in China for over 5,000 years as 'yin xing' (silver apricot). Leaves and seeds used in Traditional Chinese Medicine for respiratory conditions, urinary dysfunction, and memory decline. Introduced to Western pharmacology in the 1960s; the standardised extract EGb 761 was developed by Willmar Schwabe GmbH and remains the most extensively studied phytomedicine in Europe.

Modern research context

EGb 761 (standardised to 24% flavonol glycosides and 6% terpene lactones) is approved in Germany for symptomatic treatment of dementia and peripheral arterial disease. A 2025 meta-analysis of pooled RCT data demonstrates statistically significant benefits of EGb 761 for cognition, activities of daily living, and clinical improvement in dementia patients with a history of cerebral infarction, with a safety profile comparable to placebo [10]. Network meta-analysis confirms Ginkgo biloba among the most effective plant compounds for learning, memory, and executive function in healthy older adults [8]. A 2026 randomised clinical trial evaluated Ginkgo biloba extract for prevention of posterior capsule opacification following cataract surgery, extending its evidence base beyond neurological applications [7].

Adverse effects

GI upset (nausea, diarrhoea), headache, dizziness, and palpitations are common. Skin hypersensitivity reactions are possible. Rarely, spontaneous bleeding events including subdural haematoma and vitreous haemorrhage have been reported in case series. Ginkgolic acids in crude preparations are nephrotoxic and potentially carcinogenic; pharmaceutical extracts limit these to <5 ppm. Herb-drug interactions at the CYP450 level can precipitate adverse drug reactions through altered drug plasma levels [11].

Contraindications

Contraindicated with anticoagulants (warfarin, heparin) and antiplatelet drugs (aspirin, clopidogrel, NSAIDs) due to documented INR alteration and additive bleeding risk [4, 13]. Discontinue at least 36 hours before surgery. Caution with antiepileptics metabolised via CYP2C9, including phenytoin [6]. Avoid in patients with a history of seizures. Avoid unpurified leaf preparations containing elevated ginkgolic acids.

Clinical and phytochemical data

Used part

Leaf (standardised extract EGb 761); seed (limited therapeutic use — nephrotoxic in raw form)

Primary active compounds

  • Ginkgo flavonol glycosides (quercetin-3-rutinoside, kaempferol-3-rutinoside, isorhamnetin glycosides)

  • Ginkgolides A, B, C, J (diterpene lactones)

  • Bilobalide (sesquiterpene lactone)

  • Ginkgetin (biflavonoid)

  • Ginkgolic acids (alkyl/alkenyl phenols — limited to <5 ppm in EGb 761)

  • Proanthocyanidins

Mechanism of action

Ginkgo biloba enhances cerebral perfusion and facilitates synaptic plasticity through convergent monoaminergic and neurotrophic pathways, with documented inhibitory activity at monoamine oxidase A (MAO-A) and MAO-B [3]. The biflavonoid ginkgetin exerts pleiotropic anti-inflammatory activity through inhibition of NF-κB and suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, alongside antioxidant and antifibrotic effects via multi-target signalling modulation [9]. Ginkgolide B acts as a selective antagonist at the platelet-activating factor (PAF) receptor, inhibiting platelet aggregation. Flavonol glycosides scavenge reactive oxygen species and upregulate Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant defence genes, protecting against oxidative neuronal damage [3]. The extract modulates CYP1A2, CYP2C9, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4 activity, with clinically relevant implications for co-administered drugs [1].

Pharmacokinetics

Absorption

No human pharmacokinetic data are available for this species. The following is derived from preclinical animal studies and in vitro data only, and may not reflect human pharmacology. — Preclinical studies of Ginkgo biloba semen-derived components (GK-A, GK-2, Eucomic acid) in rats demonstrate rapid gastrointestinal absorption following oral administration [5]. Flavonol glycosides are hydrolysed in the gut by intestinal beta-glucosidases to their aglycones (quercetin, kaempferol, isorhamnetin) prior to systemic absorption; this biotransformation step is modulated by the intestinal microbiome and may affect bioavailability in dysbiotic states [2].

Distribution

No human pharmacokinetic data are available for this species. The following is derived from preclinical animal studies and in vitro data only, and may not reflect human pharmacology. — Rat pharmacokinetic studies of Ginkgo semen extract components demonstrate wide tissue distribution following oral dosing, with detection in brain, liver, kidney, and plasma [5]. Lipophilic terpene lactones are predicted to cross the blood-brain barrier based on animal tissue distribution data, consistent with the clinically observed central nervous system effects of EGb 761 [3].

Metabolism

No human pharmacokinetic data are available for this species. The following is derived from preclinical animal studies and in vitro data only, and may not reflect human pharmacology. — Rat liver microsomal studies of Ginkgo semen extract components indicate slow hepatic biotransformation, with CYP450 enzymes contributing only minimally as primary metabolising enzymes for the major active constituents [5]. However, at the whole-extract level, Ginkgo biloba modulates CYP1A2, CYP2C9, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4 activity in a clinically significant manner [1, 2], with the potential to alter plasma levels of co-administered substrates.

Excretion

No human pharmacokinetic data are available for this species. The following is derived from preclinical animal studies and in vitro data only, and may not reflect human pharmacology. — Preclinical pharmacokinetic profiling of Ginkgo semen components in rats identified renal excretion as the primary elimination route, with elimination kinetics characterised across major tissues [5]. Exact half-life values for the standardised leaf extract EGb 761 components in humans have not been captured in the peer-reviewed literature retrieved for this monograph.

Safety and interactions

Drug interactions

Anticoagulants (warfarin): Ginkgo biloba is documented to alter INR in patients on warfarin, constituting a clinically significant interaction requiring vigilant therapeutic monitoring [4]. Antiepileptics (phenytoin): A 2025 systematic review identified Ginkgo biloba as a clinically relevant interaction partner for phenytoin, with CYP2C9 modulation as the primary mechanism [6]. CYP450 substrates (CYP1A2, CYP2C9, CYP2D6, CYP3A4): Ginkgo biloba significantly modulates all four major CYP isoforms, affecting pharmacokinetics of co-administered drugs across multiple therapeutic classes [1, 2]. Antithrombotic agents (aspirin, clopidogrel, NSAIDs): Additive antiplatelet activity documented, with increased bleeding risk when combined with antithrombotic therapies [13]. CYP450-mediated herb-drug interactions can precipitate adverse events through toxic elevation of drug plasma concentrations [11].

Toxicity

Ginkgolic acids are nephrotoxic and genotoxic at elevated concentrations; pharmaceutical EGb 761 extract limits these to <5 ppm. Ginkgotoxin (4'-O-methylpyridoxine) present in seeds is seizurogenic and pyridoxine-responsive; leaf extracts contain minimal amounts. Ginkgetin demonstrates a broad preclinical safety profile across anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic applications [9]. High-dose chronic rodent studies (NTP) identified hepatocellular adenoma; clinical relevance in humans at standard therapeutic doses remains uncertain.

Special precautions

Pregnancy

Insufficient human safety data available. Ginkgolic acids are potentially teratogenic in animal models. Avoid during pregnancy.

Lactation

Excretion into breast milk is unknown. Avoid due to insufficient safety data.

Hepatic impairment

CYP1A2, CYP2C9, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4 are hepatically expressed; impaired hepatic metabolism is expected in liver disease, increasing risk of drug interactions [1, 2]. No formal dose adjustment studies have been conducted. Use with caution.

Renal impairment

Renal excretion is the primary elimination route for active metabolites based on preclinical data [5]. Accumulation of active constituents is possible in severe renal impairment. No formal dose adjustment guidelines are established.
