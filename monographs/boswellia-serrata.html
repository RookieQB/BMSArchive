Boswellia serrata

Indian frankincense / Shallaki / Salai guggul

Research areas: Inflammatory Pathways, Pain Signaling Research, Neurological & Cognitive Research, Respiratory Research

Overview · Clinical data and safety · Sources

Research overview

Historical use

Boswellia serrata resin (Shallaki / Salai guggul) has been central to Ayurvedic medicine for over 3,000 years, used primarily for inflammatory joint diseases, respiratory conditions, and gastrointestinal disorders. The Charaka Samhita and Sushruta Samhita document its application for arthritis, diarrhoea, and skin diseases. Frankincense oleoresin has been burned as incense in ancient Egyptian, Jewish, and early Christian traditions, and used topically for wound healing across Mediterranean and South Asian cultures.

Modern research context

Boswellia serrata extracts standardised to boswellic acids (particularly AKBA) are clinically established for inflammatory conditions. The strongest clinical evidence is in osteoarthritis and chronic pain: a 2025 randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled multicenter trial (Vaidya N et al.) confirmed significant efficacy in knee OA with favourable safety [6]; a 2025 observational cohort (n=103) demonstrated analgesic benefit as add-on therapy for chronic low back pain [4]. A 2025 RCT demonstrated cognitive improvement with a Boswellia-Terminalia chebula combination over 120 days [3]. Bioavailability is a central clinical challenge: a 2024 crossover pharmacokinetic trial in 20 healthy volunteers (Schmiech M et al.) showed micellar formulations achieve 1,720–4,291% higher boswellic acid bioavailability versus native dry extract, with comparable anti-inflammatory pharmacodynamics [2]. Pilot integrative oncology data suggest a survival benefit when Boswellia is combined with other phytochemicals in glioblastoma patients [8].

Adverse effects

Generally well-tolerated in clinical trials up to 12 months. Most common adverse effects: GI symptoms (nausea, diarrhoea, acid reflux) in 5–10% of participants. Rare skin reactions (pruritus, rash) in 1–3%. No significant haematological or hepatic toxicity at standard doses [4, 6].

Contraindications

No absolute contraindications established at recommended doses. Theoretical antiplatelet activity warrants caution with anticoagulants. Avoid in known hypersensitivity to Burseraceae family members. Insufficient safety data for use in pregnancy and lactation.

Clinical and phytochemical data

Used part

Oleoresin gum (standardised extract)

Primary active compounds

  • 3-O-Acetyl-11-keto-β-boswellic acid (AKBA)

  • 11-Keto-β-boswellic acid (KBA)

  • α-Boswellic acid

  • β-Boswellic acid

  • Incensole acetate (diterpene)

  • Lupeolic acid

Mechanism of action

Highly selective inhibition of 5-lipoxygenase (5-LOX) by AKBA blocks leukotriene B4 (LTB4) and 5-HETE synthesis; this 5-LOX selectivity distinguishes Boswellia from COX-inhibiting NSAIDs and accounts for its favourable gastric tolerability profile [1]. Concurrent suppression of NF-κB transcription factor reduces downstream expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) and matrix metalloproteinases [1, 4]. Modest COX-2 inhibition occurs at higher concentrations. AKBA additionally promotes ROS-mediated intracellular clearance of periodontal pathogens in gingival epithelial cells, demonstrating antimicrobial activity. Incensole acetate exerts neuroprotective effects via TRPV3 channel modulation and PPAR-γ agonism [3].

Pharmacokinetics

Absorption

Human pharmacokinetic data are available for Boswellia serrata. A 2024 crossover PK/PD trial in 20 healthy volunteers (Schmiech M et al.) compared single-dose native Boswellia serrata dry extract with a micellar (LipiSperse®) formulation [2]. Native extract showed poor oral bioavailability for all major boswellic acids (KBA, AKBA, α-BA, β-BA) due to high lipophilicity and low aqueous solubility. The micellar formulation achieved relative bioavailability increases of 1,720% to 4,291% for individual boswellic acids versus native extract, establishing formulation-dependent absorption as the primary pharmacokinetic variable [2]. A 2025 RCT of enhanced full-spectrum Boswellia extract confirmed superior bioavailability of optimised preparations in a clinical setting [5].

Distribution

High lipophilicity results in preferential distribution to fatty tissues, liver, and articular cartilage in animal models [1]. AKBA accumulates in inflamed joint tissue, consistent with its clinical efficacy in osteoarthritis. Plasma protein binding is high (>95%) for all major boswellic acids.

Metabolism

Primarily hepatic; boswellic acids undergo glucuronidation and sulfation (phase II conjugation) as principal metabolic pathways. Limited CYP450-mediated oxidative metabolism reported for AKBA at clinical concentrations [1]. Gut microbial hydrolysis of ester bonds generates deacetylated metabolites (11-keto-β-boswellic acid from AKBA). No significant CYP450 auto-inhibition or induction has been reported at standard clinical doses.

Excretion

Biliary excretion of phase II conjugates is the predominant elimination route. Renal excretion of water-soluble glucuronide/sulfate conjugates accounts for a minor fraction. Elimination half-life of AKBA is approximately 4–6 hours following administration of enhanced bioavailability formulations in human volunteers [2].

Safety and interactions

Drug interactions

Lack of clinical studies prevents interaction grading. Unknown safety profile with concomitant drug use.

Toxicity

Oral acute toxicity is low; LD50 for Boswellia extracts >2 g/kg in rodents. Across multiple RCTs and observational studies spanning OA, chronic back pain, cognition, and integrative oncology, Boswellia has been consistently well-tolerated without clinically significant liver enzyme elevations, haematological changes, or serious adverse events [3, 4, 6, 8].

Special precautions

Pregnancy

Insufficient human safety data. Traditionally avoided during pregnancy. Medicinal-dose preparations not recommended.

Lactation

No human data on excretion in breast milk. Avoid medicinal-dose supplements during lactation.

Hepatic impairment

No specific data. Hepatic glucuronidation is the primary metabolic pathway; dose reduction and monitoring may be appropriate in severe hepatic impairment.

Renal impairment

No specific data. Predominantly biliary elimination suggests renal dose adjustment is likely not required at standard doses.
