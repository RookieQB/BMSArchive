Senna alexandrina

Senna

Research areas: Digestive health, Gastrointestinal motility

Overview · Clinical data and safety · Sources

Research overview

Historical use

Senna leaf has longstanding laxative use; EMA recognizes well-established short-term use for occasional constipation [4].

Modern research context

Senna preparations have been studied for constipation, with short-term symptom benefits and important electrolyte and obstruction precautions [1][4]. A 28-day randomized trial in 90 adults compared senna, magnesium oxide and placebo; improvement favored active groups [1]. Exact senna part and anthranoid standardization were not specified in its abstract, so no product-specific dose is given here. BMS Level B provisional for constipation; a favorable OTC systematic review uses a different grading system [2]. EMA limits unsupervised leaf-medicine use to one week; the research duration is not general-use guidance [4].

Adverse effects

Abdominal cramps, diarrhea and allergic reactions can occur. Prolonged use may disturb fluid/electrolyte balance and cause reversible intestinal pigmentation; incidence is not established here [4].

Contraindications

EMA contraindications include hypersensitivity, bowel obstruction/stenosis, atony, appendicitis, inflammatory bowel disease, unexplained abdominal pain, severe dehydration, pregnancy, lactation and age under 12. Persistent constipation or symptoms suggesting obstruction require medical assessment [4].

Clinical and phytochemical data

Used part

EMA profile concerns senna leaf. Pods have related but separately specified preparations. Constipation RCT abstract does not identify plant part or sennoside content [1][4].

Primary active compounds

  • Sennosides (hydroxyanthracene glycosides); preparations are standardized, but the RCT abstract omits content [1][4]

Mechanism of action

Colonic bacteria convert sennosides to rhein anthrone; anthranoid laxative action alters colonic motility and water/electrolyte transport. Mechanism does not justify chronic unrestricted use [4].

Pharmacokinetics

Absorption

Sennosides are not absorbed or split by human enzymes in the upper gut; some metabolites are absorbed after colonic activation [4].

Distribution

Whole-preparation distribution is incompletely defined. EMA describes a small human pod-powder PK study; pod findings are not identical to a leaf-product PK profile [4].

Metabolism

Bacterial conversion to rhein anthrone occurs in the colon; circulating metabolites include conjugates [4].

Excretion

EMA describes mainly fecal elimination with some urinary/biliary metabolite elimination; no universal leaf-extract half-life is established here [4].

Safety and interactions

Drug interactions

Potassium depletion during prolonged misuse can potentiate cardiac glycosides and interact with antiarrhythmics; diuretics, corticosteroids and liquorice may increase potassium loss [4]. EMA advises medical consultation with QT-prolonging medicines and these interacting drug classes [4].

Toxicity

Fluid/electrolyte depletion is a principal misuse risk. A case report linked chronic senna exposure with cholestatic hepatitis, with confounders and no incidence estimate [3][4].

Special precautions

Pregnancy

Contraindicated in the EMA senna-leaf monograph; experimental anthranoid findings underpin precaution [4].

Lactation

Contraindicated by EMA; small quantities of active metabolites can enter milk [4].

Hepatic impairment

Suspected liver injury has been reported; no validated hepatic dose adjustment identified [3][4].

Renal impairment

Kidney disorders increase concern about electrolyte disturbances; no validated renal dose adjustment identified [4].
