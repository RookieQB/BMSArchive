Aesculus hippocastanum

Horse Chestnut

Research areas: Venous & Peripheral Circulation, Vascular Health Research

Overview · Clinical data and safety · Sources

Research overview

Historical use

Horse chestnut seed extracts have been traditionally used for pain, swelling and other complaints. EMA distinguishes specific standardized oral extracts with well-established use in venous insufficiency from traditional oral/topical preparations for minor venous complaints or bruises [3][4].

Modern research context

Standardized horse chestnut seed extracts have been studied for short-term venous-insufficiency symptoms, with larger and longer rigorous trials still needed [1]. A Cochrane review of 17 trials found signals for reduced leg pain and leg volume but emphasized caveats [1][3]. A randomized, partially blinded 12-week trial of 240 adults compared a dried seed extract with compression stockings and placebo; leg-volume findings do not establish prevention of thrombosis or equivalence for every clinical outcome [2]. Provisional archive Level B applies to studied short-term venous symptoms. Raw seeds and other raw parts are toxic and are not the tested preparations [4].

Adverse effects

Standardized oral seed extracts may cause gastrointestinal complaints, headache, vertigo/dizziness, itching or allergic reactions; EMA gives frequency as unknown. Topical preparations may cause skin hypersensitivity [3][4]. Short-term trial tolerability cannot exclude rare or delayed adverse events [1].

Contraindications

Hypersensitivity is an EMA contraindication. Sudden leg swelling, severe pain, thrombophlebitis, ulcers, skin inflammation or possible cardiac/renal insufficiency require medical evaluation rather than self-treatment [3]. Oral venous-use preparations are not recommended below age 18 in the EMA monograph. Raw seed, leaf, bark and flower ingestion must be avoided [3][4]. No validated perioperative washout interval was established.

Clinical and phytochemical data

Used part

Seed-derived standardized extracts used in clinical trials; not raw seeds, bark, leaves or flowers. EMA standardized extracts specify constituent assay and extraction solvent, so older aescin values and newer protoaescigenin-based values should not be casually interchanged [1][3][4].

Primary active compounds

  • Aescin/escin (mixture of triterpene saponins in standardized seed extracts) [1][2][3]

Mechanism of action

The exact mechanism is unknown. EMA describes preclinical and clinical pharmacological evidence involving venous tone and capillary filtration; these are pharmacological processes rather than established single molecular targets, and symptom evidence does not imply antithrombotic efficacy [3].

Pharmacokinetics

Absorption

Relevant human absorption parameters for the specified whole extract are not supplied by the EMA monograph; formulation-specific bioavailability cannot be inferred from symptom trials [3].

Distribution

Human tissue distribution and protein binding for complete standardized extracts were not established in the selected sources [3].

Metabolism

Complete human metabolic pathways and clinically relevant CYP effects were not established in selected sources [3].

Excretion

Reliable extract-level renal/biliary recovery, half-life and impairment adjustment were not established in the selected sources [3].

Safety and interactions

Drug interactions

EMA reports no interactions for the defined preparations; this is not proof that all products or drug combinations lack interactions. A clinically quantified anticoagulant or CYP interaction was not established in the selected sources, so no severity grade or washout interval is invented [3].

Toxicity

Raw seeds, bark, flowers and leaves contain a toxic component and are unsafe for oral use. NCCIH distinguishes these from standardized seed extracts in which that component has been removed [4]. The evidence concerns short exposures, often no longer than 12 weeks; reproductive, genotoxicity and carcinogenicity testing is incomplete and long-term human toxicity remains uncertain [3][4].

Special precautions

Pregnancy

Pregnancy safety is not established; EMA does not recommend use [3].

Lactation

Milk transfer and infant safety are unknown; EMA does not recommend use during breastfeeding [3].

Hepatic impairment

No validated hepatic-impairment dose adjustment or adequate disease-specific safety data identified; individualized assessment is required [3].

Renal impairment

EMA specifically calls for medical evaluation in renal insufficiency or unexplained leg oedema; no validated renal dosing adjustment is established [3].
