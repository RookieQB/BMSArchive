Plantago ovata

Psyllium / Ispaghula

For educational and scientific research only. Not medical advice. Independently verify claims against the cited primary sources. Consult a qualified healthcare professional before using any plant or fungal preparation.

Research areas: Digestive health, Lipid metabolism

Overview · Clinical data and safety · Sources

Research overview

Historical use

Ispaghula seed husk has established medicinal use as a bulk-forming laxative; this profile concerns husk, not unrelated Plantago species [4].

Modern research context

Psyllium husk has been studied for constipation, IBS symptoms and lipid biomarkers [1][2][3]. A 12-week IBS RCT compared psyllium, bran and placebo in 275 adults; psyllium improved relief in early months [1]. Fiber meta-analysis supports a psyllium signal but pooled all-fiber effects cannot be assigned wholly to psyllium [2]. A 28-trial lipid meta-analysis found lower LDL, with moderate certainty limited by inconsistency [3]. BMS Level B provisional for these specific outcomes pending full-text appraisal; cardiovascular event prevention is not established.

Adverse effects

Flatulence, distension and allergy, including anaphylaxis, can occur. Inadequate fluid can cause choking or esophageal/intestinal obstruction; powder inhalation may sensitize [4].

Contraindications

EMA contraindications include swallowing/throat disorders, GI narrowing, potential/existing obstruction, megacolon, undiagnosed rectal bleeding, persistent sudden bowel-habit change and failure to defecate after a laxative. Allergy is a contraindication [4]. Constipation persisting beyond three days or abdominal pain warrants medical advice; pediatric age restrictions depend on the indication [4].

Clinical and phytochemical data

Used part

Seed coats (ispaghula/psyllium husk), whole or powdered. Seed, husk and other Plantago species are not interchangeable [4].

Primary active compounds

  • Gel-forming mucilage polysaccharides, including arabinoxylan-type fiber; whole husk is not a purified systemic drug [4]

Mechanism of action

Water-retaining mucilage increases stool hydration/bulk; predominantly local gut action differs from systemic receptor pharmacology. Lipid-marker effects do not demonstrate prevention of cardiovascular events [3][4].

Pharmacokinetics

Absorption

Husk mucilage is minimally absorbed intact; EMA distinguishes local fiber action from uptake of degradation products [4].

Distribution

No conventional whole-husk systemic tissue-distribution profile is established; local luminal action predominates [4].

Metabolism

Limited colonic fermentation produces short-chain fatty acids; do not treat fiber as a CYP-metabolized small-molecule drug [4].

Excretion

Unabsorbed fiber and metabolites pass in feces; a conventional whole-husk elimination half-life is not applicable [4].

Safety and interactions

Drug interactions

May delay absorption of other oral medicines. EMA specifies separation and notes supervision with thyroid hormones or motility-inhibiting drugs; diabetes therapy may require review. Interaction severity depends on the medicine [4].

Toxicity

The major acute risks are mechanical obstruction and severe hypersensitivity; sufficient liquid is essential. No maximum safe dose for impaired populations is inferred from trials [4].

Special precautions

Pregnancy

EMA permits consideration when diet change fails, with limited pregnancy data; no claim of established fetal safety [4].

Lactation

EMA allows consideration when needed; specific infant-safety evidence remains limited [4].

Hepatic impairment

No hepatic dose-adjustment evidence identified for husk; impairment-specific safety is not established by these trials [4].

Renal impairment

Fluid restrictions and swallowing/obstruction risk require individual clinical assessment; no renal dose-adjustment evidence identified [4].
