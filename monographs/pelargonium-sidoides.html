Pelargonium sidoides

African Geranium / Umckaloabo

Research areas: Upper Respiratory Research, Respiratory Research

Overview · Clinical data and safety · Sources

Research overview

Historical use

Pelargonium root preparations have a documented tradition of use for respiratory complaints; the EMA common-cold indication is based on long-standing traditional use, rather than a finding of well-established clinical efficacy. The EMA monograph covers P. sidoides and/or P. reniforme [1][6].

Modern research context

Proprietary Pelargonium root extracts have been investigated for acute cough and respiratory symptoms, but certainty and formulation differences remain substantial [1][2][3]. The Cochrane review judged evidence low for acute bronchitis and very low for common cold or sinusitis, with suspected publication bias and differences between liquids and tablets [1]. A later meta-analysis of 11 EPs 7630 trials (2195 participants) reported cough improvement; the authors include manufacturer employees or paid consultants, and the analysis does not remove the limitations or establish interchangeability with other products [3]. A UK feasibility trial recruited 134 adults and demonstrated recruitment feasibility rather than definitive efficacy [2]. Archive classification is Level U for a firm clinical efficacy conclusion and Level T for EMA traditional common-cold use [1][6].

Adverse effects

EMA 2024 lists gastrointestinal complaints, nasal or gum bleeding, hypersensitivity including anaphylaxis, rash, pruritus, urticaria, angioedema, hepatitis and hepatotoxicity, with frequency unknown [6]. The Cochrane trials reported more adverse events with extract than placebo, without serious events in those trials [1]. Liver causality remains disputed: a case reassessment found none of 15 reports probable or highly probable, but this does not negate current regulatory warnings [4][6].

Contraindications

Hypersensitivity is an EMA contraindication. Signs of liver injury require stopping the product and medical assessment [6]. The 2024 monograph does not recommend oral use below age three. For its dry-extract preparations b) and c), children under six should receive only liquid dosage forms; a dry extract and a solid finished dosage form are different concepts. Symptoms persisting longer than one week or worsening require medical advice [6]. No evidence-based perioperative washout interval was established in this extraction.

Clinical and phytochemical data

Used part

Root; clinical findings mainly concern proprietary EPs 7630 liquid/dry extracts. EMA describes defined ethanol-extracted preparations and also covers P. reniforme, so genus-level and mixed-species preparations cannot be assumed equivalent to P. sidoides trial material [2][3][6].

Primary active compounds

  • Root extract is a mixture; no single clinically proven active constituent accounts for the symptom findings [1][3]

Mechanism of action

Experimental work summarized by the EPs 7630 review investigates host-defense signaling, including interferon and tumor necrosis factor, alongside antiviral and mucociliary effects. These are primarily preclinical hypotheses; they do not establish antiviral treatment efficacy, prevention of complications or a clinically validated molecular target [3].

Pharmacokinetics

Absorption

Human constituent-specific absorption and absolute bioavailability were not established in the selected clinical sources; EMA traditional-use monograph does not provide pharmacokinetic parameters [1][6].

Distribution

Human tissue distribution and protein binding of whole root extracts remain insufficiently characterized in the selected sources [6].

Metabolism

Complete human metabolic pathways and clinically relevant CYP effects were not established in the selected sources [6].

Excretion

Validated whole-extract half-life, renal clearance and impairment adjustments are unavailable in the selected sources [6].

Safety and interactions

Drug interactions

EMA reports no interactions, which is not proof that all combinations are safe [6]. Nasal and gingival bleeding are reported adverse reactions; a clinically quantified interaction with anticoagulants or antiplatelets was not established in this extraction. Medication review remains prudent [6].

Toxicity

Hepatitis and hepatotoxicity reports remain in current EMA warnings even though causality was not substantiated in one reassessment [4][6]. A manufacturer-sponsored, non-interventional 2026 study enrolled 300 adults (298 completing) and reported no clinical signs of liver injury after a seven-day PELARGO capsule course. Short follow-up and symptom-based surveillance cannot rule out rare, delayed or asymptomatic injury [5]. Product-specific findings cannot establish long-term safety of all root extracts.

Special precautions

Pregnancy

Safety in pregnancy is not established and EMA does not recommend medicinal use [6].

Lactation

Milk transfer and infant safety are unknown; EMA does not recommend medicinal use during breastfeeding [6].

Hepatic impairment

No validated impairment adjustment identified. Reported hepatitis/hepatotoxicity warrants particular caution in existing liver disease and immediate medical assessment of possible liver-injury symptoms [6].

Renal impairment

No validated renal-impairment adjustment or adequate disease-specific safety data identified in selected sources [6].
